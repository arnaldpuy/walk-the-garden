Why the panels disagree

Every analysis of raw data requires choices: which observations count as outliers, which covariates enter the model, which functional form to fit, how to operationalise the outcome. Each choice is a fork, and four binary forks already open sixteen paths. A conventional study walks one path — often selected after seeing the data — and reports its endpoint as the result. Gelman and Loken called this the garden of forking paths: no dishonesty is required, yet the reported estimate is conditional on a route the reader never sees.

A multiverse analysis, in the sense of Steegen and colleagues, inverts the logic. It enumerates the defensible pipelines, runs them all, and treats the resulting distribution as the finding. In the illustration above, the single pipeline reports β = 0.22 with a tidy confidence interval, while the sixteen-pipeline multiverse spans −0.07 to 0.29 with a median of 0.10 — and the published estimate (outlined in ochre on the right) is revealed as one draw from that spread, not a property of the data alone. Reporting the distribution turns analytical uncertainty from a hidden liability into an explicit, inspectable part of the evidence.
